# DINOv3 Reads Strips, V-JEPA 2 Watches Surf

For the NDBC buoy-camera strips, the best-fitting backbone is Meta's self-supervised **DINOv3** (web-image weights), with the **ViT-B/16** as the working model. Run it frozen first, read the patch tokens of all six views with a small attentive probe, then adapt it gently with linear-probe-then-fine-tune (LP-FT) or LoRA. For the WebCOOS shore video, the pick is **V-JEPA 2 ViT-L** fed **64 frames at 4 fps** (a 16-s window) from a fixed surf-zone crop, frozen with an attentive probe; keep the 1B ViT-g-384 for the final run. The deciding principle is that both tests are out-of-distribution: unseen buoys, unseen days, unseen sites. Under that kind of shift, the evidence favours the model whose frozen features already encode geometry, fine texture and motion, adapted lightly rather than retrained. DINOv3 leads every frozen probe that resembles wave-height regression. Its distilled ConvNeXt-Tiny alone cuts the ImageNet ConvNeXt-Tiny's frozen depth error from 0.666 to 0.448, which makes the DINOv3 ConvNeXt-T swap now training (Bar B1) the right controlled first step. V-JEPA 2 is the only open video encoder pretrained on windows long enough to hold a wave period, and it leads frozen motion benchmarks by about 4–27 points. Expect modest gains. In published wave studies a backbone swap moved scores by 0.1–2 points, while splits, temporal context and pretraining moved them by 7–60, and the project's own seed-to-seed spread is larger than any published backbone effect. Ocean-SAR and Earth-observation foundation models supply no usable features for oblique RGB cameras. Swell is a data problem, not a backbone problem. The buoy camera and its horizon motion both follow the wind sea, so swell has to come from ERA5 on the buoys. On the shore video, a pixel-spectrum front end beside the video encoder is the direct way to capture it. No published study has compared modern foundation models on camera wave height against instruments, so the first probe comparison is itself a new result.

## A backbone swap moved published wave scores 2 points; the split moved them 60

The four papers the project is measured against all chose their backbone by habit, not by test, and their own numbers show why that barely mattered. **Choi et al. 2020** compared VGG, Inception v3, ResNet and DenseNet on NDBC buoy-camera views (lower half of the image, 224², calm vs rough). Over 10 replicates, VGG16 reached **84.05 % against 83.89 % for ResNet**, and dropping ImageNet weights cost about 7 points (about 77 %). The split was random, with the same buoys and days in both sets ([Choi et al. 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oceaneng.2020.107129); [project notes](../docs/literature/papers/choi2020.md)). **Kim et al. 2023** went from 0.783 with a snapshot CNN to 0.904 with GoogLeNet features fed to a BiLSTM over 180-s clips, and to 0.932 with augmentation. That is a **12-point gain from temporal context**, but frames from the same hour sat in both training and test ([Kim et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.oceaneng.2023.116002); [project notes](../docs/literature/papers/kim2023.md)). On a ship-bridge camera, ResNet-101 and ViT-B/32 scored 0.974 and 0.955. A ResNet trained on one dataset and tested on another fell from **0.88 to 0.28** ([Vorkapić et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2407.03755)). Five CNNs for active wave breaking landed within 0.873–0.882 of one another ([Stringari deepwaves](https://github.com/caiostringari/deepwaves)). Read together: architecture is worth about 0.1–2 points, pretraining about 7, temporal context about 12, crop placement about 9, and the camera domain about 60.

The evidence at unseen sites, which is the project's actual test, points the same way. On river cameras, a fine-tuned ImageNet ResNet-50 trained on 86 cameras and tested on **4 cameras never seen in training** estimated water level about as well as a gauge up to 50 km away, using labels standardised per gauge ([Vandaele et al. 2023](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/environmental-data-science/article/calibrated-riverlevel-estimation-from-river-cameras-using-convolutional-neural-networks/912B6F25D567B59C38C6D76185829F76)). The same group earlier found "no clear dominance" of any CNN, with camera location and occlusion mattering more ([Vandaele et al. 2021](https://hess.copernicus.org/articles/25/4435/2021/)). The only held-out-site result in wave video is **Kamagata et al. 2026**, and it is weak. Held-out Hs has RMSE 0.586 m, R² 0.107, Pearson 0.436 and a **regression slope of 0.12**, meaning predictions collapse toward the mean. It rests on a single seed, and its labels cannot be traced to an instrument ([Kamagata et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11998v1); [project notes](../docs/literature/papers/kamagata2026.md)). Its "V-JEPA ViT-S" is named `vit_small_patch16_224`, which is timm's identifier for an ImageNet-supervised ViT-S, while the official V-JEPA release contains only ViT-L/16 and ViT-H/16 ([facebookresearch/jepa](https://github.com/facebookresearch/jepa)). Its windows of 16 frames at 10 fps also cover 1.6 s, less than one wave period. Treat it as a baseline to re-run, not as evidence about V-JEPA. Yang et al. 2026 is simulated and offers no backbone evidence. Its real-data analogue on the buoys is image plus horizon attitude, which the project can already measure.

The deciding property is therefore robustness under shift, and the transfer literature is specific about which regime delivers it. Across 10 distribution-shift datasets, including satellite imagery with geographic shift, **full fine-tuning gained about 2 % in distribution but lost about 7 % out of distribution** against a linear probe. LP-FT (train the head first, then fine-tune everything) gained about 10 % out of distribution over plain fine-tuning ([Kumar et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.10054)). On VTAB-1K, carefully tuned LoRA and adapters beat both linear probing and full fine-tuning on all 19 tasks. Full fine-tuning of DINOv2 under a shared hyperparameter grid collapsed on the quantity-like tasks closest to wave height: **Clevr counting 27.3 vs 89.8 with LoRA, KITTI distance 47.1 vs 83.8** ([Mai et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.16434)). The buoy dataset has about 246k views but only 67 training buoys, which is low-shot in exactly the dimension that matters, camera diversity. The project's own runs show the size of the noise: two seeds of the same v0 recipe differed by **0.116 macro-F1 on the test buoys** ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). Any backbone comparison that lacks multiple seeds and buoy-level confidence intervals will measure noise.

## DINOv3 leads every frozen image probe that resembles wave-height regression

Wave height from a buoy camera is a metric regression on geometry and texture: crest height against the horizon, whitecap fraction, surface roughness. The closest public proxies are frozen depth regression (NYUv2 RMSE), dense segmentation (ADE20k), texture classification (DTD) and corruption robustness (ImageNet-C). In the table below, "frozen" means the backbone's weights are fixed and only a small head is trained.

Table values come from the DINOv3 paper's Tables 3, 7, 14 and 15 ([DINOv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10104v1)), the DINOv2 paper ([DINOv2](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07193)), the SigLIP 2 paper ([SigLIP 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.14786)) and the licence pages cited below. Protocols differ between papers, so compare numbers only within one source. ADE is segmentation mIoU (higher is better); NYU is the error of a depth head on frozen features (lower is better); ImageNet-C is corruption error (lower is better).

On geometry, the ordering is consistent: **DINOv3 > RADIO ≈ PE-spatial > DINOv2 ≫ SigLIP 2 > PE-core/CLIP > MAE**. At ViT-B size, DINOv3's frozen depth error is 0.373, against 0.416 for DINOv2, 0.512 for SigLIP 2 and 0.641 for PE-core. A frozen DINOv3 7B depth head matches a fine-tuned Depth Anything v2 (ARel 4.3 vs 4.4), which led the authors to say fine-tuning "is not necessary" on their benchmarks ([DINOv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10104v1)). A dense attentive-probing study of 18 backbones, run before DINOv3, already ranked DINOv2 first ([Lüddecke & Ecker 2025](https://mlanthology.org/tmlr/2025/luddecke2025tmlr-characterizing)). The 2023 Battle of the Backbones result, that supervised CNNs "still perform best on most tasks", predates DINOv3 and is superseded for dense work ([Goldblum et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.19909)). The one place language supervision wins is texture: OpenCLIP-G scores 86.0 on DTD against 84.5 for DINOv2-g ([DINOv2](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07193)). That earns SigLIP 2 ViT-B a slot as a contrast arm but not the primary role. MAE is poor frozen (12-dataset linear average 78.0, iNat21 32.3), and I-JEPA has no published probe numbers and an archived repo ([MAE repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/mae); [I-JEPA repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/ijepa)). Neither belongs in the comparison.

The evidence on frozen probes versus fine-tuning for small domain datasets divides by how small and local the signal is. On underwater imagery (AQUA20), **logistic regression on frozen DINOv3 ViT-B/16 features reached 91.5 % macro-F1, above a fully supervised ConvNeXt at 88.9 %**, and still 81.8 % with 13 images per class ([Rost et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.00313)). On chest radiographs, DINOv3 was the strongest initialisation at 512 px, especially as ConvNeXt-B, which beat ViT-B/16 under both full and parameter-efficient fine-tuning. The gains were largest for small focal findings, and 1024 px rarely helped ([Tayebi Arasteh et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.07191)). In industrial inspection, a frozen DINOv3 ConvNeXt showed "no clear advantage" over a supervised ImageNet ConvNeXt, but was the better starting point for fine-tuning on RGB tasks ([Gharbage et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.23472)). The protocol lesson is sharper than the backbone lesson. A linear probe on the CLS token under-reads models whose information is spread across patch tokens ([Psomas et al. 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.10178)). Re-evaluating DINOv2-g on Kinetics with an attentive probe instead of a linear one raised it from 78.4 to 83.4 ([V-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.08471)). That **5-point probe effect exceeds every published wave-backbone gap**. Whitecaps and short wind-sea texture are small, focal signals, so the fair frozen comparison is an attentive probe over patch tokens, not a CLS linear probe.

For robustness, DINOv3 has the best published ImageNet-C error: **19.6 against 22.7 for PE-core, 24.1 for DINOv2 and 30.0 for SigLIP 2** at the largest sizes. Its distilled ConvNeXt-T lifts ImageNet-R from 45.0 to 73.7 over the supervised ConvNeXt-T. At 512 px the supervised model degrades (ObjectNet 44.5 → 27.1) while the DINOv3 version improves (52.6 → 58.7) ([DINOv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10104v1)). That matters because the 480×270 views already exceed the 224–256 px at which supervised checkpoints were trained. Robustness also grows with size: DINOv2's ImageNet-C error falls from 54.4 (S) to 42.7 (B), 31.5 (L) and 28.2 (g) ([DINOv2](https://arxiv.org/html/2304.07193)). DINOv2-L embeddings drift less than CLIP's under JPEG compression (0.038 vs 0.084), though foundation models overall "generally lack robustness to common perturbations" ([Liu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2604.14973)). **No benchmark covers twilight, sun glint or tilted horizons**, so robustness on the project's twilight strips has to be measured directly, by binning errors by solar elevation.

Resolution and compute favour the ViTs at this image size. A 480×270 view at patch 16 becomes a 30×17 grid of 510 tokens, against about 15×8 at the end of a ConvNeXt. DINOv3 keeps stable feature maps from 896×512 up to 3584×2048 (ViT-S+), so upsampling views 1.5–2× to resolve distant whitecaps is a cheap, legitimate test ([DINOv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10104v1)). DINOv3's RoPE position encoding accepts the native 16:9 shape, so there is no reason to centre-crop away the horizon's long axis ([DINOv3 repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov3)). If DINOv2-L or larger is used, the four-register variants remove artefact tokens that sit in low-information background, the analogue of sky and flat sea here ([Darcet et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2309.16588)). Scaling the timm RTX 4090 benchmark to the project's view size, one frozen pass over all ~246k views takes roughly 5 min for ViT-B, 15–20 min for ViT-L and 2–3 min for ConvNeXt-T, and an H200 should be no slower. These are estimates, not measurements ([timm benchmark](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/huggingface/pytorch-image-models/main/results/benchmark-infer-amp-nchw-pt240-cu124-rtx4090.csv)). Even a frozen ViT-7B pass is about an hour in 13.4 GB of bf16 weights, but fine-tuning it would need about 107 GB of optimiser state, which is impractical on a shared GPU. Caching all ViT-B patch tokens in fp16 takes about 193 GB, so cache pooled tokens. On licences: DINOv3 permits commercial use but bans military and ITAR uses, requires acknowledgement in published research, and gates downloads ([DINOv3 licence](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov3/blob/main/LICENSE.md)). DINOv2, SigLIP 2 and PE are Apache-2.0 ([SigLIP 2 weights](https://huggingface.co/google/siglip2-so400m-patch16-naflex); [PE repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/perception_models)). MAE and ConvNeXt V2 weights are non-commercial ([ConvNeXt V2 repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/ConvNeXt-V2)).

### The buoy-strip pick: DINOv3 ViT-B/16, with B1 as the controlled step

The recommendation is **DINOv3 ViT-B/16, web weights** (`facebook/dinov3-vitb16-pretrain-lvd1689m`, torch.hub `dinov3_vitb16`), used frozen with an attentive probe over the patch tokens of all six views, then adapted with LP-FT or LoRA. Four reasons support it. It holds the largest frozen-geometry lead at a size that allows five-seed adaptation runs on a shared H200. Its 30×17 token grid per view gives the probe the local detail that small focal signals need. Its native aspect ratio keeps the horizon intact. And it is the regime (frozen, then gentle adaptation) that the out-of-distribution evidence favours. A cross-attention probe over 6×510 tokens is the frozen counterpart of the current transformer over six view embeddings. The project has found that the six views are taken at separate moments: horizon roll barely agrees between adjacent views (correlation 0.03–0.04). Pooling across them therefore averages up to six independent looks at the sea ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)).

The strongest case against this pick is the ConvNeXt evidence. In chest X-rays and industrial inspection, DINOv3 ConvNeXts did best when fine-tuned, and ConvNeXt-B beat ViT-B. That is exactly why **Bar B1**, the same ConvNeXt-T and recipe with only the weights changed, is the right first step: it isolates the effect of the weights. One caveat governs how to read it. B1 reuses arm A's full fine-tuning recipe (10 epochs), which was tuned for ImageNet weights, and Mai et al. show that a grid tuned for one initialisation can wreck a self-supervised one. If B1 misses its bar, the conclusion is "not with this recipe", not "DINOv3 does not help", until LP-FT (`--lp-epochs`) and a lower backbone learning rate have been tried. The pick changes to DINOv3 ConvNeXt-T, which is cheaper and a drop-in replacement, if the fine-tuned B1 beats the ViT-B probe-and-adapt arms on the validation buoys by more than the seed spread. It changes to SigLIP 2 if the texture arm wins.

Whichever wins, **judge it on Hs and wind-wave height, not swell**. The project's analysis found that the camera's calls follow wind-wave height (slope 0.68) far more than swell height (0.37) ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). No backbone will see low-steepness swell in a snapshot, so swell should come from ERA5.

On motion, the V-JEPA 2 family leads frozen probes clearly: **SSv2 73.7–77.7, against 69.7 for InternVideo2-1B, 56.1 for VideoMAE V2-g and about 50 for image models**. Frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-g-384 (77.3) matches fully fine-tuned VideoMAE V2-g (77.0) ([V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09985); [VideoMAE V2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.16727)). With few labels the gap widens. At 5 % of SSv2 labels a frozen V-JEPA-H scores 54.0 against 28.0 for VideoMAE V2-g ([V-JEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.08471)). On fluid-dynamics parameter estimation, a JEPA trained on 10 % of the data beat VideoMAE trained on all of it (MSE 0.57 vs 0.67) ([Qu et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.13227)). The closest analogue to periodic wave motion is EchoJEPA, a V-JEPA 2-style model adapted to cardiac ultrasound. Frozen with an attentive probe, it cut ejection-fraction error to 4.26 against 5.33 for the best domain baseline, beat a matched pixel-MAE by 26.7 %, and generalised across hospitals (5.44 vs 6.71) ([EchoJEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02603)). The steelman for the other side comes from Google's SciVid benchmark. There, pixel-reconstruction models did best on continuous geophysical fields: **4DS-L had the lowest typhoon-pressure RMSE (3.84 vs 4.16 for V-JEPA-H)**, and VideoMAE V2-g was best on WeatherBench2 ([SciVid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03578)). A pixel-MAE arm is therefore a necessary contrast, not a formality. Intuitive-physics benchmarks (IntPhys 2) leave every model near chance ([Meta blog](https://ai.meta.com/blog/v-jepa-2-world-model-benchmarks/)), but they test object permanence, not oscillating fluids, so they say little here.

The decisive difference is the time window. V-JEPA 2 pretrains on 16 frames at 256 px and then cools down on **64 frames at 384 px, both sampled at 4 fps**. Its authors found no gain from going beyond 64 frames ([V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09985); [cooldown config](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/blob/main/configs/train/vitg16/cooldown-384px-64f.yaml)). Sixteen seconds spans 1–3 periods of a 5–15 s sea, and 4 fps is about ten times the Nyquist rate for swell. Most other encoders see 8–32 frames covering 1–4 s. Kamagata's 16 frames at 10 fps covered 1.6 s, a likely contributor to its low held-out skill ([Kamagata et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.11998v1)). The project's clip cutter currently takes **64 frames at 2 fps (32 s), letterboxing the full 2688×1520 frame into 448×336** ([webcoos_video_clips.py](../scripts/webcoos_video_clips.py)). The recommendation differs on two points.

On frame rate, 2 fps covers more periods per window, but it doubles each V-JEPA 2 tubelet (the two-frame time step a token spans) from 0.5 s to 1 s, away from the time scale the frozen encoder learned. A 4–10 min clip already gives 15–37 sixteen-second windows for a record-level pooling step to combine. So 4 fps should be the primary input and 2 fps a pre-registered ablation; no source measures V-JEPA 2 accuracy against input frame rate, so this is a judgement. On field of view, shrinking 2688 px to 448 px means each 16-px token covers roughly 100 source pixels or more, which erases small distant breakers. A fixed per-camera crop of the breaking and swash zone at 256–384 px keeps them, and the full frame can stay as a context arm. Both changes are cheap ffmpeg re-cuts.

Kamagata's SlowFast head suggests a principled second arm. Read "SlowFast" as two input rates: 64 frames at 4 fps for period, plus 32 frames at 10–20 fps for breaking onset, fused in the probe.

Compute is manageable if the probe is sized to the data and features are cached sensibly. One 64-frame window at 256 px costs about **11.5 TFLOP for ViT-L and 114 TFLOP for ViT-g at 384 px**, and going from 16 to 64 frames costs 6–7×, not 4×, because attention cost grows with the square of the token count. For about 75k windows (3,000 records × 25), a frozen pass is roughly 0.6–6 GPU-hours for ViT-L and 6–56 for ViT-g-384. These are estimates bounded from measured A100 throughput, not H200 measurements ([Hiera](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.00989)). The real constraint is storage. One layer's full token map is 17 MB per window for ViT-L, about **1.3 TB per layer** across 75k windows, so cache per-time-step pooled tokens or run the backbone online during probe training. Meta's own attentive probe has about 50M parameters on ViT-L, estimated from Hugging Face totals. That is oversized for a few thousand records, so use 1–2 blocks at width 256–384.

### The shore-video pick: V-JEPA 2 ViT-L on a surf-zone crop at 4 fps

The recommendation is **V-JEPA 2 ViT-L** (`facebook/vjepa2-vitl-fpc64-256`), frozen, fed 64 frames at 4 fps from a fixed surf-zone crop. Its tokens come from three or four intermediate-to-late layers, as in Meta's multilevel probe (layers at about 60, 75, 87 and 100 % depth). A small attentive probe with one query each for Hs, Tp and direction reads them, and a second attentive pooling over the 15–37 windows of each record produces the 30-min estimate the buoy reports ([V-JEPA 2 eval config](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2/blob/main/configs/eval/vitg-384/diving48.yaml)). The case for it is that it is the only open, permissively licensed encoder trained at the right time scale. It leads every frozen motion benchmark, and its family degrades least with few labels. It also loads in Hugging Face Transformers with a clear MIT licence, and at ViT-L it is cheap enough for multi-seed sweeps on a shared GPU. Escalate to ViT-g-384 for the final run. Escalate to V-JEPA 2.1 if localising breaking matters, since its dense loss nearly halves ViT-g's frozen depth error (0.642 → 0.350) at almost no motion cost ([V-JEPA 2.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.14482)).

Two outcomes would change this pick. If the pixel-MAE contrast (VideoMAE V2-g or 4DS-L) beats V-JEPA 2 on validation days, switch to it, as SciVid's weather results suggest it might. If shuffling the frames costs nothing, the model is reading appearance, not motion, and a per-frame DINOv3 is enough.

Physics also predicts an asymmetry worth planning for. Wave frequency is conserved as waves shoal, but height is not. A CDIP buoy 20–30 km offshore therefore constrains Tp at the pier far better than Hs, which shoaling, refraction and breaking alter site by site. Expect Tp to generalise to unseen sites better than Hs, and consider giving the model each site's metric scale (pixels per metre at the breaker line) as an input.

## Ocean and Earth-observation models teach a lesson but supply no features

The ocean-SAR foundation models are the only ones trained on sea-state imagery, and on their own radar data the generic DINOv3 already matches them. On Sentinel-1 Wave Mode, OceanSAR-2 (a ViT-S/16 of about 21M parameters, accepted at EUSAR 2026) was compared with DINOv3, WV-Net and the generalist EO model TerraMind ([OceanSAR-2](https://arxiv.org/html/2601.07392v1)):

| Model | Hs error, zero-shot kNN on frozen features (m) | Hs error, 3-layer MLP head (m) |
|---|---|---|
| OceanSAR-2 | 0.52 | 0.40 |
| DINOv3 (generic) | 0.55 | **0.39** |
| WV-Net (ResNet-50, SimCLR) | 0.64 | 0.427 |
| TerraMind (generalist EO) | 0.73 | 0.67 |

WV-Net adds a second lesson. Its linear probe (Hs RMSE 0.500 m, against 0.601 for an ImageNet ResNet-50) beat its own end-to-end fine-tuning (0.530) ([WV-Net](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.18765v1)). That is Kumar's frozen-beats-fine-tune effect in a sea-state regression. The OceanSAR-2 authors attribute TerraMind's last place to the absence of ocean SAR in its pretraining and to its MAE objective, which gives "less linearly separable features". None of these models transfers as a feature extractor, however. Their input is single-channel calibrated radar backscatter over 20-km, near-nadir vignettes, and the sea signal comes from radar imaging physics. An oblique RGB camera about 3 m above the sea sees specular sky, sun glitter and foam under strong perspective, so the two share no low-level statistics. Weights are also patchy. OceanSAR-1's four 23.6M checkpoints are public on Hugging Face (licence not shown, linear-probe Hs RMSE 0.63 m) ([galeio-research](https://huggingface.co/galeio-research)), but OceanSAR-2 and WV-Net weights were not confirmed as released.

General EO models are a worse bet. No study has tested Prithvi, DOFA, SatMAE, Scale-MAE, Clay, CROMA or DINOv3-SAT on oblique, ground-level or camera imagery. The nearest robustness benchmark covers overhead imagery only ([RSPDBench](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.23427), search summary). Prithvi-EO-2.0 (Apache-2.0, 100–600M) is built around multispectral, multi-temporal satellite bands ([Prithvi-EO-2.0](https://huggingface.co/ibm-nasa-geospatial/Prithvi-EO-2.0-600M)). Even on remote sensing's own tasks, domain pretraining beats ImageNet by only "up to 2%" ([arXiv 2307.01645](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2307.01645), search summary). DINOv3's web 7B model equals or beats its satellite sibling on GEO-Bench (81.6 vs 81.1 classification) ([DINOv3](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.10104v1)). SatMAE and Clay licences and weights were not researched. The only EO test worth its cost is DINOv3-SAT ViT-L as a one-line frozen-probe swap, and the expected result is no gain.

The physics and flow front ends belong in the design for a different reason: they carry information that no backbone can recover from a snapshot. For the buoy strips, optical flow and timestacks are impossible, because each view is a single moment. Two scalar channels are available. The first is **horizon tilt**: the project's de-mounted roll and pitch track the buoy's own spectral slope with a within-buoy Spearman correlation of **0.56 [0.54, 0.58], positive at 75 of 76 buoys** ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)), and ship-borne horizon trackers reach 0.3–0.6° pitch and roll accuracy ([JTECH 2015](https://journals.ametsoc.org/view/journals/atot/32/1/jtech-d-14-00047_1.xml)). The second is **whitecap fraction** from an adaptive brightness threshold, with the caveat that published wind exponents for whitecap coverage range from 1 to 5 ([Kubryakov et al. 2021](https://data-ww3.ifremer.fr/BIB/Kubryakov_etal_RSE2021.pdf)). Both are CPU-cheap and late-fused next to the backbone embedding and ERA5. Neither fills the swell gap. The horizon motion correlates with wind-wave height (0.56) and hardly with swell height (0.05), because long swell barely tilts the hull, so ERA5 remains the swell source.

For WebCOOS video, the best value per cost is a **pixel time-series spectrum** (cBathy-style FFT over sampled pixels or timestack lines). It gives peak frequency and swell-band energy with no training. Second is **timestack crest tracking**: a ResNet-18 U-Net trained on only two annotated timestacks reached 0.17 m depth RMSE in the surf zone ([Lange et al., WaveCrestDetection](https://github.com/AthinaLange/WaveCrestDetection)), and a streaming webcam's timestacks gave 0.43 m depth RMSE from wave celerity ([Andriolo et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs17132274)). Dense learned flow comes last. SEA-RAFT is BSD-3 licensed and "at least 2.3x faster" than prior methods ([SEA-RAFT](https://github.com/princeton-vl/SEA-RAFT)), but no flow method has been validated against a buoy. In the surf zone, flow on unfiltered frames also mixes wave-phase motion with foam advection ([Remote Sensing 13:1874](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs13101874)). Kamagata's period model shows the limit of the flow-and-physics route without instruments. Its ViViT reached 0.77 s RMSE, but it was scored against expert labels after training on unvalidated optical-flow pseudo-labels, and its physics penalty's valid period range is quoted three different ways ([Kamagata et al., arXiv 2606.13302](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.13302v1)). Physics losses on dispersion, steepness and period bounds are free to add as regularisers but have no instrument-validated benefit. Rendered-sea pretraining carries a known shortcut risk: a pose model trained on renders with an infinite horizon became biased in camera height ([arXiv 2406.09260](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.09260)).

## About an hour of frozen probes settles the choice before long training runs

The cheapest decisive experiment caches frozen features once on the lab GPU server and trains every probe on the cache. Fine-tuning is reserved for the top two arms. For the buoy strips, keep the 12 test buoys sealed. Make every choice (probe type, layer, learning rate, epoch, LoRA rank) on validation buoys drawn from the 67 training buoys. Because probes cost seconds, also run 5-fold cross-validation grouped by buoy, so the rankings rest on more than a dozen sites; spatial and grouped blocking is the standard defence when samples are dependent ([Roberts et al. 2017](https://nsojournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/ecog.02881)). Extract at native 480×272 (476×266 for patch-14 models) without square crops. Store the CLS token and mean-pooled patch tokens per view (under 1 GB for ViT-B), plus 2×2-pooled patch tokens for the attentive probe (about a quarter of the 193 GB full-token cache).

| Arm | Backbone (weights) | Regime | What it isolates |
|---|---|---|---|
| A (exists) | ConvNeXt-T, ImageNet | Full fine-tune, transformer over six views | Current baseline (v2 arm A) |
| B1 (training) | ConvNeXt-T, DINOv3 | Arm A's recipe unchanged | The weights alone |
| P1–P5 | ConvNeXt-T ImageNet; ConvNeXt-T DINOv3; DINOv2-reg4 ViT-B/14; **DINOv3 ViT-B/16**; SigLIP 2 ViT-B/16 | Frozen: ridge on [CLS ‖ mean patch] across six views; 2-layer MLP; attentive probe over 6×N tokens | Feature quality without adaptation; size of the probe effect |
| P6 (optional) | DINOv3 ViT-L/16; DINOv3-SAT ViT-L/16; DINOv3 ViT-B at 1.5–2× input | Frozen, best probe | Scale; web vs satellite; whitecap resolution |
| F1 | Best two from P | LP-FT from the best probe head, per-backbone learning-rate sweep, layer decay for ViTs, non-zero drop-path | Gentle full adaptation |
| F2 | DINOv3 ViT-B/16 | LoRA rank 8–16, head initialised from the attentive probe | Parameter-efficient adaptation |

The frozen stage costs well under an hour of GPU time by the estimates above, plus CPU time for the probes. A fine-tuning arm costs about 450 times one feature pass (30 epochs × 5 seeds × about 3 for the backward pass). Fine-tuning is therefore where the shared H200's time should go, and only for arms that earned it on validation buoys.

Report RMSE in metres for Hs and wind-wave height, balanced accuracy on the wind-sea classes, and the **regression slope** of predicted against observed values, to expose the Kamagata-style collapse toward the mean. Also report the gap between seen and unseen buoys: Kumar's prediction is that frozen and LP-FT arms narrow it even when their in-distribution score is lower. Bin the unseen-buoy errors by solar elevation (day vs civil twilight). Use at least 3 seeds for probes and 5 for fine-tuned arms, varying initialisation, data order and augmentation together, which approaches the ideal variance estimate at a fraction of the cost ([Bouthillier et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03098)). Compare arms by paired differences under a bootstrap that resamples whole buoys. Fix in advance the smallest difference worth acting on, as Bar B1 already does.

The **decision rule** is this. The arm with the lowest validation-buoy wind-wave RMSE, averaged over seeds, goes to the sealed test against arm A under a bar written before it runs. If every foundation-model probe falls within seed spread of the ImageNet ConvNeXt-T probe, the backbone is not the bottleneck. In that case effort moves to more buoys, calm-sea months and fusion.

For WebCOOS, split by blocks of days with an embargo between them, and hold out whole cameras. Oak Island East and West, Kamagata's test cameras paired with CDIP 200, make the natural held-out site. Run five arms on the same records. V0 is the pixel-spectrum scalars alone with a ridge head, the physics floor. V1 is per-frame DINOv3 ViT-B, frozen and pooled over time, which reads appearance only. V2 is frozen V-JEPA 2 ViT-L at 64 frames and 4 fps on the surf-zone crop, with the multilevel attentive probe and record-level pooling. V3 is V2 fused with the V0 scalars. V4 is V2 on the existing 2 fps full-frame cut. Every V2 run carries two controls, shuffled frames and a single repeated frame, which the video-probing literature recommends for checking that temporal order is actually used ([Punzo et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09646)).

To beat Kamagata fairly, also re-run its own recipe on these records: 16 frames at 10 fps, 224 px saliency crops, with both an ImageNet ViT-S and V-JEPA 2-L in its backbone slot. Score everything with its measures (Hs RMSE, R², Pearson, slope; Tp RMSE; direction RMSE). Then add Kim's five-class average-height task, using H_ave ≈ 0.63·Hs ([project notes](../docs/literature/papers/kim2023.md)). Escalate in this order if V2 plateaus: ViT-g-384, V-JEPA 2.1, the VideoMAE V2-g or 4DS-L contrast, then LoRA at rank 16–32. In SciVid, LoRA at rank 32 came within one point of full fine-tuning (58.4 vs 59.3 on point tracking) ([SciVid](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.03578)).

## Conclusion

The question that looked like "which backbone" turns out to be "which training regime under site shift". The project's 67 buoys and handful of shore cameras make camera diversity, not image count, the scarce resource. In that setting the literature consistently rewards strong frozen features with gentle adaptation, and the measurable difference between good backbones sits below the noise of a single training run. Bar B1 is therefore necessary but not sufficient. It answers whether better weights help under the current recipe, while the frozen-probe ladder answers whether the recipe itself is what keeps the camera from generalising. The same logic explains why the most valuable additions are not backbones at all. Swell is invisible to a buoy snapshot whatever encoder reads it, and the hull's tilt misses it too. On the buoys it belongs to ERA5; on the shore, to a pixel spectrum.

The comparison is publishable regardless of which arm wins. No instrument-labelled study has put DINOv3 against an ImageNet CNN on buoy cameras at unseen buoys, or a verified V-JEPA-family encoder against shore video with buoy truth at unseen sites. Kamagata's "V-JEPA" result cannot be traced to a V-JEPA checkpoint, and its windows are shorter than a wave period. A properly windowed V-JEPA 2 run at Oak Island, with shuffled-frame controls, would therefore be the first clean test of the claim Kamagata's paper set out to make. Beyond that, the largest untested lever is continued self-supervised pretraining on unlabelled WebCOOS footage, which the project has in abundance. Domain adaptation of this kind is what lifted EchoJEPA over every off-the-shelf baseline on another periodic-motion video domain.
