# Squeeze More From Each Camera First

Spend the next round on what surrounds the encoders, not on new encoders. Ranked by expected value for this project's data, the additions are: (1) cross-validation by buoy group with five-seed ensembles and calibrated intervals, so each decision rests on all 79 buoys instead of 12; (2) a pooling head that reads every 16-second window of each 4–10-minute shore clip instead of one; (3) a fusion head that takes the wind sea from the camera and the swell from ERA5; (4) physics front ends for the video, chiefly a pixel-intensity spectrum for wave period; (5) a period-and-direction head on the buoy stills; and only after those, (6) continued self-supervised pretraining on the project's own footage and (7) larger video encoders. The deciding principle is that compute is plentiful and independent cameras are scarce, so an addition earns its rank by getting more out of each labelled camera (more of its record, the part of the sea it can actually see, comparisons that rise above seed noise), not by being newer or bigger. One correction to the premise: ConvNeXt-Tiny (distilled from DINOv3, fine-tuned gently) is the **buoy-still** model, best so far at an H_s error of **0.440 m** at 12 unseen buoys (H_s: significant wave height, roughly the mean height of the highest third of the waves; RMSE: the typical miss, in metres). The shore-video plan already uses a frozen **V-JEPA 2 ViT-L**, and nothing released through November 2026 clearly beats it, so "more out there" for video means contrasts and escalations, not a better default. Compute does not separate the project from the four papers it must beat. Their models top out near 0.14 billion parameters and roughly 0.6 trillion multiply-adds per input, Kamagata's eight-GPU DGX A100 is the only hardware the project's notes record for any of them, and a slice of one shared H200 exceeds what any of them needed. Ranks 1–5 together cost about 100 H200-hours; only rank 6 would use hundreds. Three project results set the order: once each backbone had a suitable recipe, swapping backbones moved H_s by only about 0.01 m while every bar was decided by twelve test buoys; one 16-second window holds one to three waves, far too few to pin down a height or a period; and the camera beats ERA5 on the wind sea yet adds nothing to the total height, which swell dominates at the test buoys. The order changes if grouped cross-validation shows backbone gaps wider than the seed spread, if whole-record pooling fails to beat a single window, or if domain pretraining lowers unseen-buoy error by more than the seed spread; that last result would move it to the top.

## Seven additions, ranked by what each labelled camera yields

The project's binding constraint is not GPU time but the number of independent seas it can learn from and be judged on. The buoy model trains on 55 buoys, picks its epoch on 12 more, and is judged at 12 it has never seen; the shore experiment has four camera views at three sites ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md); [video design](../docs/experiments/webcoos_video.md)). Three results from the past week show what that scarcity does. **First, once each backbone had a recipe that suited it, the backbone moved H_s error by about 0.01 m (0.440–0.455 m), while the recipe moved it by up to 0.11 m and one change of random seed moved v0's test macro-F1 (a 0–1 score of hits and false alarms averaged over the sea-state classes) by 0.12.** Second, the validation buoys carry much less swell than the test buoys, which skews every choice made on them. Their swell share (the fraction of wave energy that is swell) is 0.40, against 0.67 at the test buoys, and the ImageNet-gentle arm duly ranked best on validation and worst on test. Third, the camera reads the wind sea, not the swell. Its wind-wave height error is **0.215 m against ERA5's 0.341 m**, but adding it to ERA5 and the buoy's own wind leaves total-H_s error unchanged at **0.258 against 0.259 m** ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). An addition therefore ranks high when it extracts more from each labelled camera, points the camera at what it can see, or makes a comparison decidable. It ranks low when its main promise is a stronger encoder. In the table, T_p is the peak period, the seconds between waves at the peak of the wave spectrum.

| Rank | Addition | What it fixes in this data | Cost on the shared H200s | Experiment that decides it |
|---|---|---|---|---|

| 2 | Record-level pooling of all 16-s windows in each 4–10 min shore clip (attention pooler on cached V-JEPA 2 tokens) | One window holds 1–3 waves: H_s sampling error near ±50 %, T_p unresolvable | Encoder ≈ 1–7 GPU-hours for 3,000–10,000 records (estimate); video decoding is the real cost | New bar: pooled minus single-window RMSE of H_s and T_p, same date folds, against mean and energy-mean baselines |

The review also called continued pretraining on WebCOOS footage the largest untested lever. B1–B3 and the controlled studies below demote it to sixth.

## One shared H200 out-computes all four target papers

None of the four papers was limited by hardware; each was limited by labels and protocol. The table sets their models beside this project's. Compute per input is in GMAC (billions of multiply-add operations to process one input) or TMAC (trillions); parameters are the network's learned weights.

| Work | Model | Parameters | Compute per input | Hardware (as recorded) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Choi 2020, buoy stills | VGG16, ImageNet weights, one view at 224² | 138 M | 15.5 GMAC per view | not recorded |
| Choi 2020, ship video | frame differences → VGG → bidirectional ConvLSTM, 16 frames | VGG-sized (≤ 140 M) | ~250 GMAC per clip (estimate) | not recorded |
| Kim 2023 | GoogLeNet features → BiLSTM over up to 360 frames (180 s) | 6.6 M + ~48 M (estimate) | ~560 GMAC per 180-s clip (estimate) | not recorded |
| Yang 2026 | ResNet-18 per frame + attitude MLP + attention, 7–9 frames (simulation) | ~12 M | ~13–16 GMAC per window | not recorded |
| Kamagata 2026 | ViT-S frame encoder + two-layer SlowFast transformer, 16 frames | ~22 M + ~7 M | ~74 GMAC per clip | NVIDIA DGX A100, 8 × 40 GB |
| This project, buoy stills | DINOv3 ConvNeXt-Tiny, six 270 × 480 views | 29 M | ~69 GMAC per strip (estimate) | one shared H200, 40–55 min per run (measured) |
| This project, shore video | V-JEPA 2 ViT-L, frozen, 64 frames as two 256-px squares | 300 M | ~11.5 TMAC per clip (estimate) | one shared H200, ~1.5 s per clip with decoding (measured) |

Per-image figures come from torchvision's model table, whose "GFLOPS" column counts multiply-adds ([torchvision](https://docs.pytorch.org/vision/stable/models.html)). Architectures and hardware come from the project's full-text notes, which record no GPUs for Choi, Kim or Yang ([Choi](../docs/literature/papers/choi2020.md); [Kim](../docs/literature/papers/kim2023.md); [Yang](../docs/literature/papers/yang2026.md); [Kamagata](../docs/literature/papers/kamagata2026.md)). The estimates scale those figures to each input size. Kim's BiLSTM size assumes 1,024-wide GoogLeNet features, and the ConvNeXt figure scales 4.46 GMAC at 224² to the 270 × 480 views. The video figure takes the earlier review's 11.5 TFLOP per 64-frame window and counts the two squares each frame is fed as. Model sizes for DINOv3 and V-JEPA 2 are from the official repositories ([DINOv3](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov3); [V-JEPA 2](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2)). **The largest model in the four papers has 0.14 billion parameters; the project's video encoder alone has 0.3 billion and spends about 150 times Kamagata's compute per clip.** No official V-JEPA checkpoint at ViT-S size exists, so Kamagata's "V-JEPA ViT-S" cannot be traced to a V-JEPA release ([facebookresearch/jepa](https://github.com/facebookresearch/jepa)).

The hardware gap is just as wide and just as beside the point. An H200 has 141 GB of memory at 4.8 TB/s and about 989 TFLOPS of dense bf16 arithmetic (NVIDIA's headline 1,979 assumes sparsity) ([NVIDIA H200](https://www.nvidia.com/en-us/data-center/h200/)). A 40 GB A100 has 312 TFLOPS dense ([A100 datasheet](https://www.nvidia.com/content/dam/en-zz/Solutions/Data-Center/a100/pdf/nvidia-a100-datasheet-us-nvidia-1758950-r4-web.pdf)). **One H200 therefore does the arithmetic of about three of the A100s in Kamagata's DGX, and the lab server's eight together come to about three whole DGX A100s.** The DGX was far larger than Kamagata's model needed. One pass over its 894 training windows is roughly 66 TMAC forward, about one to two seconds of a single A100 including the backward pass (an estimate; their CPU optical flow would have dominated) ([arXiv:2607.11998](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.11998)). The "HPC-enabled" in that title describes the machine, not the workload. What capped all four papers was data and protocol. Choi had 20 buoys over nine weeks, split at random. Kim had 30 hours at one camera. Yang's data were simulated, and Kamagata trained on six scenes. This project has 41,091 labelled strips at 79 buoys and a shore archive that keeps 4–10-minute clips back to at least March 2024 ([benchmark targets](../docs/literature/benchmark_targets.md)).

The real constraint on the lab server is sharing, which caps free memory at 16–117 GB per GPU. Mixed-precision Adam needs about **16 bytes of model state per trained parameter** before activations, and activation checkpointing trades about a third more compute for much less activation memory ([ZeRO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1910.02054)). By that rule, 16 GB free is enough for frozen inference of anything up to the DINOv3 7B (13.4 GB of bf16 weights), for full fine-tuning of ConvNeXt-Tiny, ViT-B or ViT-L at modest batch sizes, and for LoRA-style continuation of a ViT-L. 40–80 GB adds V-JEPA 2 ViT-L continuation at its official per-GPU batch and full fine-tuning of the 1-billion-parameter ViT-g. Even 117 GB cannot fully fine-tune the 7B, whose optimiser state alone is about 107 GB; that would need its state split across four or more partly free GPUs. Nothing in the ranking needs it. Ranks 1–5 add up to about 100 H200-hours, most of it the grouped folds of rank 1, and rank 6 would add roughly 60–230 more on dedicated GPUs. Budget for tuning on top of that: in the most careful published continued-pretraining study, development cost about seven times the final runs (34,673 against 4,947 GPU-hours) ([FINO](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05107)).

## Twelve test buoys cannot separate models a centimetre apart

The backbone bars show the problem. B1 failed for a reason that had nothing to do with the backbone: full fine-tuning washed out DINOv3's features. The other two ended with intervals that straddle zero. B2 missed by a hair: DINOv3-gentle minus arm A was **−0.010 m, with a 95 % interval of [−0.024, +0.001]**. That interval comes from a bootstrap over whole buoys: it shows how far the difference would move had other buoys been drawn, and while it includes zero the comparison is undecided ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). With 12 test buoys, intervals stay this wide whatever the model. With validation buoys carrying far less swell than the test buoys, the epoch and arm chosen on validation are chosen where swell matters least. More seeds narrow only the seed part of that noise. The buoy part narrows only with more buoys.

The training approach that fixes this is **cross-validation over the 43 buoy groups the station split already defines**. Buoys within 300 km of each other are chained into one group. In five folds, each buoy is predicted once by a model that saw neither it nor its neighbours, and every fold mixes swell climates. The project log already names this as the step that "would use every buoy as a test buoy once" ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). Bootstrapping over 79 buoys instead of 12 should shrink intervals by roughly the square root of the ratio, about 2.6 times. That is an estimate, but at that width B2's difference would likely have cleared zero. Folds will be uneven, because the US West Coast group alone holds 21 buoys. The sealed 12-buoy results stay the headline; the grouped folds become the protocol for choosing and comparing. Out-of-fold predictions are also the honest inputs for any fusion head: scikit-learn's stacking trains its combiner on cross-validated predictions for exactly this reason, and GroupKFold keeps a buoy inside one fold ([scikit-learn stacking](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/ensemble.html#stacking); [GroupKFold](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/modules/generated/sklearn.model_selection.GroupKFold.html)).

Ensembles and distribution heads belong to the same step, because they turn seed noise into a measured quantity. A five-member deep ensemble is the simple, scalable baseline that beat MC dropout in its original tests ([Lakshminarayanan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.01474)), and the project already averages three seeds. Each member should predict a mean and a spread. The plain Gaussian likelihood loss down-weights hard points, which β-NLL corrects by weighting each point by its predicted variance to a power β ([Seitzer et al., ICLR 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.09168)). Deep evidential regression should not be the main method: its mathematical foundation has gaps, and its epistemic uncertainty is only relative ([Meinert et al., AAAI 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.10060); [Jürgens et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.09056)). For intervals, no method is calibrated at an unseen buoy by construction. The valid version calibrates on held-out buoys, which gives correct coverage on average over new buoys if buoys are exchangeable draws from one population ([hierarchical conformal prediction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.06342)). It gives no guarantee at any single buoy. Weighted conformal prediction does not help here, because it corrects only covariate shift ([Tibshirani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06019)). Report coverage (the share of buoy values inside the stated 90 % interval; 0.90 is right) per buoy and per sea-state bin. Report CRPS too: the average error of the whole predicted distribution, in metres, which equals the absolute error for a single-number forecast ([Gneiting & Raftery](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)). A calibrated distribution also yields Choi's and Kim's class probabilities directly, as the probability that H_s falls in each bin. That makes a separate ordinal head unnecessary.

The cost is known from measurement: one buoy-strip run takes **40–55 minutes on one shared H200**, so five folds with three to five seeds cost about 15–25 GPU-hours per arm. The deciding experiment re-runs arm A and DINOv3-gentle under grouped cross-validation, with B2's bar written down beforehand and scored on the paired difference over all 79 buoys. If the backbone gap still sits inside the seed spread there, backbones are settled for the strips and effort moves down this list.

## A 16-second window cannot measure a 30-minute sea

Sampling statistics, not network design, set the first limit on shore video. **For a 17-minute record, the uncertainty of significant height is about ±12 % and of peak frequency about ±5 %, at 90 % confidence** ([Donelan & Pierson 1983](https://earthref.org/ERR/46206/)). Error shrinks with the square root of record length. Scaling that figure down to one 16-second window gives roughly ±50 % in H_s at one standard deviation; the standard theory, worked through in the research behind this report, puts it at about 40–60 % depending on wave period. Period is worse. A 16-second window resolves frequency only in steps of 1/16 Hz, so on frequency content alone it cannot tell a 10-second sea from a 12-second one. It holds one to three waves, and any period skill it shows must come from priors such as wavelength in the image or the site's usual climate. Two caveats cut the other way. A camera sees an area spanning many wavelengths, which adds samples a point record lacks, and no source quantifies how many. The label is noisy too: one buoy record's 90 % interval is about −10 % to +15 % of H_s ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). Finite-record noise also biases measured storm peaks upward, which argues for smoothing labels over adjacent records ([Forristall et al. 1996](https://ascelibrary.com/doi/10.1061/(ASCE)0733-950X(1996)122:5(216))).

The papers line up with that arithmetic. **Kim's accuracy rose 12 points when the input grew from a snapshot to 180 seconds**, and Kamagata's 1.6-second windows produced a held-out R² of 0.107 (R²: the share of ups and downs in the true H_s the estimate reproduces; 1 is perfect, 0 is no better than the average) ([benchmark targets](../docs/literature/benchmark_targets.md)). The project's fixed design takes **one 16-second window around each clip's midpoint**, while the archive holds 4-minute clips at Currituck and 10-minute clips at Oak Island ([video design](../docs/experiments/webcoos_video.md); [benchmark targets](../docs/literature/benchmark_targets.md)). That is 15–37 non-overlapping windows per clip, and all but one go unread. The earlier review proposed pooling them; the design that was fixed did not.

The fix is a record-level head on cached features, not a longer encoder. V-JEPA 2 gained 0.7 points from pretraining on 64 rather than 16 frames and **nothing from 128 or 256** ([V-JEPA 2](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.09985v1)). Feeding a whole 10-minute record to one ViT-L would mean about 300,000 tokens and roughly 80 times the windowed cost, with no evidence of benefit. The proven alternatives are small and permutation-invariant. One is attention-based multiple-instance pooling, a learned weighted average of window embeddings ([Ilse et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1802.04712)). Another is the Set Transformer's learned-query pooling ([Lee et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.00825v2)), or a Perceiver's cross-attention ([Jaegle et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2103.03206v2)). The closest physical-regression template is EchoJEPA, which keeps a V-JEPA 2 encoder frozen and concatenates the tokens of many clips, tagged by clip, into one attentive probe ([EchoJEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02603)). Replace "clip" with "window position" and that is the head this project needs. The same evidence also sets the baselines it must beat. Simple averaging of clip estimates reached R² 0.81 for cardiac ejection fraction ([EchoNet-Dynamic](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8979576)), so the learned pooler must beat both the mean of per-window log H_s and the mean of per-window H_s². The second is the physically correct average, because H_s measures wave energy. With 3,000–10,000 records, the simplest pooler that beats those means is the one to keep.

The cost sits in decoding, not in the network. Running ViT-L over every window of 3,000–10,000 records is about 1–7 dedicated GPU-hours of encoder arithmetic (estimate). The measured 1.5 seconds per clip covers four arms on one window and is mostly decoding 290 MB, 2,592 × 1,944 files ([video design](../docs/experiments/webcoos_video.md)). Cut each clip once into 4-fps, 256-pixel frames on the server, with GPU decoding where possible, and keep only the cut frames: 10,000 raw Jennette clips would fill about 2.9 TB. The current pooled-token cache (8 × 4 × 4 tokens over four layers) is about 1 MB per window per square, roughly 0.3–0.7 TB for every window of 10,000 records, which fits the server's data disk; one token per time step cuts it to about 64 KB per layer. The pooler then trains in minutes. **The deciding experiment is a new bar set beside M1 and M2: record-pooled minus single-window V-JEPA 2, RMSE of H_s and of T_p, interval below zero by the same bootstrap over dates, with Kim's five classes reported for single windows, 180-second spans and whole clips.**

Physics front ends (rank 4) are cheap partners for the pooled encoder because they read minutes of record directly. Wave period survives shoaling and refraction while height does not. A period measured near the pier should therefore match the offshore CDIP buoy better than a height, and a pixel-intensity spectrum over minutes measures it with no training. The mature learned tools work on timestacks, pixel lines sampled through time. A ResNet-18 U-Net trained on two annotated timestacks recovered surf-zone depth from crest speed to 0.17 m, with public weights ([WaveCrestDetection](https://github.com/AthinaLange/WaveCrestDetection)). Active breaking is classified at about 90 % balanced accuracy (the hit rate averaged over its classes) with public code ([Stringari et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7878786/)). Runup networks were trained on more than 7,000 timestacks from six sites ([Buckley et al. 2024](https://www.usgs.gov/publications/wave-runup-and-total-water-level-observations-time-series-imagery-several-sites)). The classical spectral method needs about 33 hours of observation to reach 0.51 m depth error and is not robust in storms ([cBathy evaluation](https://www2.whoi.edu/staff/elgar/wp-content/uploads/sites/153/2018/03/135.pdf)), a reminder that these are period and bathymetry tools, not height tools. The test is two arms on the same records: the spectrum alone with a ridge head, the physics floor, and V-JEPA 2 plus the spectrum. Both are scored on T_p against CDIP and set beside Kamagata's held-out T_p error of 1.42 s.

Is there "more out there" than V-JEPA 2 for the video encoder itself? Not yet as a better default. A search of June–November 2026 found no successor from a major lab: no V-JEPA 3, no DINOv4. It found no released encoder with public weights that beats V-JEPA 2's frozen motion probes. SSv2, an action benchmark that cannot be solved from appearance alone, is the usual check; V-JEPA 2 ViT-g at 384 px gets 77.3 % of its clips right ([V-JEPA 2 repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2)). Two research encoders are worth watching for weight releases. JEM exceeds DINOv3 on panoptic segmentation at 7B scale with 12 times less data ([JEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.10013)). VANE gains 7.1 points over V-JEPA 2.1 on EPIC-KITCHENS action anticipation ([VANE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.30724)). Video-generator features are not the answer. In the most systematic frozen-probe comparison, V-JEPA scored 95.0 % on the MVP physics test and the diffusion model's probes consistently trailed ([Punzo et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.09646)); one contrary report beats V-JEPA on two plausibility benchmarks without naming its models ([arXiv:2606.05328](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.05328)). One signal does favour a different family. On continuous geophysical fields, pixel-reconstruction encoders won, with VideoMAE V2-g and 4DS best on WeatherBench 2 ([SciVid](https://arxiv.org/html/2507.03578v1)). So the escalation order, if rank 2 plateaus, is V-JEPA 2 ViT-g-384 (1 billion parameters, 2 GB of bf16 weights, about ten times ViT-L's cost per clip), then V-JEPA 2.1 for its denser features, then a VideoMAE V2-g or 4DS contrast.

## The camera should hand ERA5 a wind sea, not a total

At the test buoys swell carries about two thirds of the wave energy, so total H_s is mostly a swell number, and swell is what ERA5 carries and the camera cannot see. A better wind-sea estimate can move only the third of the energy that is wind sea. That fits Test 3, where the camera cut ERA5 + wind's wind-wave error by 30 % and left total H_s level ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). The fusion literature offers another explanation for such failures: a combiner trained on in-sample camera predictions learns to over-trust the camera. It does not apply here, because Test 3's ridge was fitted on validation buoys the camera never trained on. Two other explanations do fit: those buoys' low swell share, and a linear combiner, which cannot add wind-sea and swell energies the way the sea does. ERA5 also partitions the sea differently from NDBC: its swell error (0.361 m) exceeds its total error (0.267 m). A good combiner should therefore keep ERA5's total and replace only its wind-sea share, not trust ERA5's own swell split ([benchmark targets](../docs/literature/benchmark_targets.md)).

The closest published design comes from satellite radar. Wang et al. 2026 let a learned wind-sea height set a JONSWAP prior spectrum, then fused it with the observed swell spectrum through a U-Net, reaching **H_s RMSE 0.40 m against ERA5**. The authors note that buoy validation is still missing ([Wang et al., Remote Sensing 2026](https://doi.org/10.3390/rs18111703)). A cautionary result comes from directional spectra. A network trained to correct an analytical prior beat pure learning in-domain, but analytical methods stayed most accurate and the learned part was fragile on unseen seas ([Afham & Chandrasena](https://engrxiv.org/preprint/download/6980/11389/9697)). Two design rules follow. Build the fused height from ERA5's total with its wind-sea energy swapped for the camera's, assuming NDBC's partition energies add; check that against NDBC's processing documentation before treating it as a hard rule. And add a learned gate that falls back to ERA5 when the camera is uninformative (dusk, haze, glare). Fancier fusion is optional. Across image-plus-table benchmarks, late fusion and plain concatenation are hard to beat ([multimodal bag of tricks](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.16243)), and feature-wise conditioning helped only modestly in medical imaging ([DAFT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2107.05990)). FiLM and cross-attention between views are ablations, not the main bet. A buoy-identity embedding is out, since it cannot generalise to an unseen buoy. Tabular foundation models such as TabPFN-3 fit 40,000 rows, but their weights are licensed non-commercially ([Prior Labs](https://docs.priorlabs.ai/models)). Their calibration benchmarks test only data drawn like the training set, never a shift to new sites ([TabReliBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2610.13654v1)). TabPFN can be one candidate combiner beside ridge and gradient boosting, nothing more.

The test costs CPU minutes on the out-of-fold predictions rank 1 produces. The bar mirrors Test 3: total and wind-wave RMSE against the ERA5 + wind ridge, scored over grouped folds. Because the 12 test buoys have already served Tests 2, 2b and 3, confirmation should come from **strips archived after the package was built**, which accumulate hourly. That turns a follow-up finding into a confirmed one.

Rank 5 extends the same idea into the targets the project names but does not yet predict. NDBC's spectral summaries, which label every strip, also carry wind-wave and swell periods and a mean direction. The project already reads the buoys' full spectral-density records for its horizon analysis ([experiment log](../docs/experiments/ndbc_buoycam_sea_state.md)). A head that predicts the wind-sea part of the 1-D spectrum, scaled by a separately predicted energy, would give H_s, period and mean period from its moments. That split mirrors the best radar inversion, a U-Net predicting a normalised spectrum on a 24 × 32 wavenumber-direction grid ([Tripathi et al., ICASSP 2024](https://archimer.ifremer.fr/doc/00885/99670/)). **No learned spectral head from optical cameras was found, so a buoy-validated one would be new.** Direction needs each view's compass heading, which the burned-in caption carries and the package cuts from the pixels; it must be read first and given as an input. Direction should be predicted as sine and cosine, which beat raw angles in ship-motion work ([DTU study](https://orbit.dtu.dk/en/publications/on-the-determination-of-the-relative-wave-direction-based-on-meas/)). No study shows multi-output training improving height for image inputs, so judge the extra outputs on their own errors, with H_s checked for harm. Each seed costs about one GPU-hour. The comparison is ERA5's period and direction at the same unseen buoys.

## Domain pretraining is affordable but has slipped to sixth

Continuing a foundation model's self-supervised training on the project's own unlabelled images is well within reach. The question is whether it pays here. The best controlled evidence comes from FINO, by DINOv3's authors, who continued DINOv3 ViT-L on unlabelled domain images with weak, label-free metadata. Scores on frozen features (each benchmark's own 0–100 measure) rose by 19 points on microscopy and 6 on chest X-rays, domains far from web photos. They rose by only 1.5 on wildlife camera traps, a domain web photos already cover, and fell 1.4 there with plain continued training. **Supervised fine-tuning hurt out-of-distribution scores in the same study**: the camera-trap score fell from 51.4 frozen to 43.4, and the satellite worst-region score from 45.0 to 40.5 ([FINO](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05107)). ExPLoRA continued DINOv2 with low-rank updates plus one unfrozen block. It lifted a satellite linear-probe accuracy from 69.0 % to 77.5 % but lowered camera-trap accuracy from 66.0 % to 63.0 %, and its authors call gains modest below about 50,000 images ([ExPLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10973)). A chest X-ray scaling study beat its open weights only from about 100,000 images, and with 3.5 million images it forgot: its score on another hospital's images fell from 60.2 to 57.7 ([RAD-DINO scaling](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.12818v1)). For video, the echocardiography model continued from V-JEPA 2 ViT-L on 525,000 clips but never reported the frozen V-JEPA 2 baseline ([EchoJEPA](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.02603)). A lung-ultrasound study that did compare them found **a −0.16-percentage-point difference and concluded that "the limiting factor is the labelled cohort rather than the encoder"** ([arXiv:2610.03290](https://arxiv.org/abs/2610.03290)).

Read against this project, that evidence is mixed. Shore-camera stills are ordinary outdoor photos that web pretraining almost certainly covers. Buoy strips are low-resolution, tilted and often hazy, which puts them further out, and B3's result shows a real gap: frozen DINOv3 features alone fell 0.111 m short. But B1–B3 also showed that once any reasonable backbone is trained gently, features move H_s by about 0.01 m. The labelled set of 55 training buoys looks like the limit, exactly as in the lung-ultrasound null. Continued pretraining is the label-free way to close the frozen-feature gap. Whether it beats gentle fine-tuning, which already closes most of that gap, is unknown, and no study has tried it on sea-surface imagery. Hence sixth: worth one clean pair, not a programme.

The compute is modest next to original pretraining, which took 22,000 A100-hours for DINOv2's largest model ([DINOv2](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.07193)). ExPLoRA's ViT-L runs took 96–320 GPU-hours on one workstation card or four small ones, and FINO's smaller domains took 11–161 H100/H200-hours ([ExPLoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.10973); [FINO](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.05107)). Scaled to an H200, an ExPLoRA-style continuation of DINOv3 ViT-B or ViT-L on 250,000–1,000,000 buoy views is an estimated **30–150 dedicated GPU-hours**; ViT-B costs about a third of ViT-L. Continuing V-JEPA 2 ViT-L for 10–20 million clip samples is an estimated **30–80**. Both multiply by two to three on a busy GPU. The data clear the roughly 100,000-image threshold: the 41,091 kept strips hold about 237,000 usable views. FINO's metadata guidance maps onto label-free fields the project already stores: station, view index, time of day, season. Using wave labels as metadata would make the run supervised. FINO's code is on a branch of the DINOv3 repository. DINOv3's training launcher assumes a SLURM cluster, though, so it needs adapting to torchrun inside Docker, while V-JEPA 2's launcher runs single-node directly ([DINOv3 repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/dinov3); [V-JEPA 2 repo](https://github.com/facebookresearch/vjepa2)). The deciding experiment continues DINOv3 ViT-B on the buoy views, re-runs B3's gentle recipe from the continued weights, and compares the two under rank 1's grouped folds. If it wins by more than the seed spread, distil it into ConvNeXt-Tiny with DINOv3's public distillation code and move pretraining up the list. The video counterpart waits for enough unlabelled clips cut on the server.

## Long-video, tracking and depth models do not fit this data

State-space and memory video models solve a problem this project does not have. VideoMamba uses about 40 times less memory than TimeSformer at 64 frames, but larger pure-Mamba models overfit and needed distillation ([VideoMamba](https://arxiv.org/html/2403.06977v2)). Memory transformers extend temporal reach cheaply, and MeMViT's code is non-commercial ([MeMViT](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2201.08383.pdf)). With frozen V-JEPA 2 tokens, a whole record is only 15–37 windows, trivial for an attention pooler, so these models matter only if a backbone must read minutes of raw frames end to end. That would mean retraining a weaker backbone on a few thousand labels. One cheap exception deserves a side arm after rank 2: per-frame pooled features fed to a small Mamba or GRU over the full record at 4–20 fps. In remote heart-rate sensing, a periodic regression of the same shape, RhythmMamba's error stayed flat from 2.6 to 60 seconds of video and rose sharply at 1 second ([RhythmMamba](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.06483v2)).

Measurement models built for solid scenes fail on water. No point tracker has been validated on waves or foam. The nearest evidence is river-surface velocimetry, where learned optical flow roughly matched particle-image methods ([DPRI abstract](https://www.dpri.kyoto-u.ac.jp/hapyo/26/pdf/C204.pdf)). Surf-zone foam is advected by bores, and glints move with the sun, so tracks need calibration and physics before they mean height. AllTracker (MIT licence, dense, fits 40 GB) is the one to try later; CoTracker3 is non-commercial ([AllTracker](https://github.com/aharley/alltracker); [CoTracker](https://github.com/facebookresearch/co-tracker)). Monocular depth and feed-forward 3-D models now output focal length and camera tilt from one image. None estimates camera height above the sea, none has been tested on maritime scenes, and depth networks are known to be fooled by water reflections ([arXiv:2404.07176](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07176v1)). Metric wave elevation still requires stereo; ship-borne stereo reaches H_s within about ±10 % of buoys ([Sensors 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8220/26/3/993)). Some WebCOOS cameras re-point from month to month ([video design](../docs/experiments/webcoos_video.md)), so any metric calibration would follow coastal practice: per-view ground control points plus horizon stabilisation ([UCalib](https://doaj.org/article/a4fc27e698434beeac46bb6812ab5c92)). Use learned depth on the land only, as a check. The other exclusions appear in earlier sections: evidential heads, buoy-identity embeddings and ordinal heads beyond a fairness check on Kim's classes, plus video-generator features and tabular foundation models as the main combiner. A frozen 7B image encoder on the strips is out as well, because B3 left even frozen DINOv3 ViT-B 0.111 m behind a trained ConvNeXt, and fine-tuning the 7B needs about 107 GB of optimiser state.

| If this happens | Then the order changes this way |
|---|---|
| Grouped cross-validation shows a backbone gap larger than the seed spread | Encoder work (ranks 6–7) moves above ranks 3–5 for the strips |
| Whole-record pooling does not beat a single window or the energy mean | The camera's area already supplies the samples; crop, resolution and encoder become the video levers |
| Partition-aware fusion does not beat ERA5 + wind on total H_s under grouped folds | The camera's product is a wind-sea instrument where no wave model or anemometer exists (ships, coasts), not an H_s upgrade at buoys |
| Continued pretraining lowers unseen-buoy error beyond the seed spread | Domain pretraining moves to rank 1 for both cameras, and the video continuation runs next |
| Shuffled-frame video scores as well as ordered video | Motion is unused; a per-frame DINOv3 replaces V-JEPA 2, and rank 2 pools per-frame image features instead of video windows |
| JEM or VANE release weights with a frozen-probe win | They enter as a one-line encoder swap at rank 7 |

## Conclusion

The project's question has shifted from "which network" to "how many independent seas does each decision rest on, and how much of each record does the model read". Every result so far points the same way. The backbone is worth a centimetre. Seed luck and the choice of twelve buoys are worth more than that. And the camera's real advantage is a wind-sea measurement that a wave model lacks, not a better total height. Architecture work should therefore be judged by information per labelled camera. Measured that way, a pooling head that reads all fifteen to thirty-seven windows of a clip is a better bet than any encoder released this year.

That reframing also changes what "beating" the four papers means. Choi's number is already passed on a harder split. Kamagata's is passed on buoy cameras, with the like-for-like Oak Island video test still to come. Yang's attitude gain did not survive real buoys. Kim's numbers are the ones left open, and the larger one is a record-length result: twelve points came from reading 180 seconds instead of one frame. A shore-video model that reads whole instrument-labelled records at held-out cameras would test Kim's claim at the length where it was made, and no published wave paper has done that. The H200s make it cheap; what it needs is the whole record, not a bigger model.
